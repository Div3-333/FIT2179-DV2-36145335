From Sky to Tap

Australia's weather patterns are rapidly shifting away from historical norms. As rain-bearing winds fail and intense heat drives up evaporation, Victoria's traditional water infrastructure is struggling to keep up.

The Rainfall-Deficit Gradient

This spatial view shows a sharp rainfall-deficit gradient across Victoria. The deep purple south-west is still below normal, but the orange north-west and pale yellow Mallee cells carry the largest losses. Together, these bands locate the current centre of the systemic rainfall deficit: a north-western hotspot grading into central Victoria.

A Century of Heat & The Seasonal Fever

The temperature profile has shifted upward in the ACORN-SAT monthly means. The 116-year heatmap shows warmer recent bands, while the seasonal comparison makes the change explicit. Every month in the 1910-1920 baseline is cooler than the matching 2010-2020 comparison window, with the largest lifts in January, March, October and November.

The Atmospheric Engine

The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) gives the rainfall bars their climate setting. Negative phases, including the 2015 El Nino period, align with several dry stretches in Caulfield rainfall. The pattern is strongest at seasonal scale: ENSO pressure shapes the rainfall regime, while monthly rainfall still carries local variability.

The Catchment Toll

When rainfall weakens and heat intensifies, reservoirs respond slowly. Read as a sequence, the next charts show how repeated dry months translate into delayed storage stress.

The Evaporation Squeeze

The modern record shows annual evaporation generally rising through time, with 2025 reaching the highest plotted evaporation value. The orange trendline summarises that upward long-run movement. The hottest years, especially 2009 and 2019, sit high on the evaporation axis while their rainfall bubbles are small. Together, the encodings reveal a compound-stress pattern: several high-heat years also combine high evaporation with low annual rainfall.

The Multi-Year Deficit

This composite view reveals the lag effect. During the 2006-2009 Millennium Drought peak, a cluster of rainfall deficits coincides with the reservoir storage line falling to its lowest point. Storage then takes until 2012 to climb back above 80%, and it never reaches 100% within the 2004-2019 window shown here.

The Reservoir Network

This map compares each reservoir's storage percentage with its capacity. High-storage reservoirs sit near the top of the colour scale, while lower-storage reservoirs need closer attention. The large Thomson bubble matters because it is the biggest reservoir in the dataset at 1,068,000 ML and sits at 74.5% full, so a moderate percentage there represents a much larger volume than the same percentage in a small basin.

The Unseen Buffer

Surface reservoirs are only one part of Victoria's water system. The groundwater map shifts attention to bore-permit density, showing where pressure on hidden aquifers is concentrated.

Subterranean Stress

This high-resolution grid reveals critical bore-density zones where 10 or more permits fall inside a 0.1-degree grid cell. Permit concentration becomes the pressure signal: purple cells mark lower-density areas, while the yellow-orange cluster marks the strongest visible groundwater-pressure signal.

The Proportional Pivot

This normalized stacked bar converts annual water-resource volumes into proportional composition. At 2020, desalinated water appears as a small but visible emergency layer, while groundwater remains the larger alternative supply. Toggle the "Full Cycle" to see the repeated post-pulse composition for 2023-2025, where surface water returns to roughly three quarters of the total and desalination drops back below 1%.

Engineering Survival

When natural storage weakens, Melbourne can add manufactured supply through desalination. These final views show when that engineered option became large enough to alter the system.

The Desalination Pulse

To read this dashboard, compare the grey demand silhouette with the blue desalination pulse. The data shows that in 2020-21 and 2021-22, desalination supplied about 28-29% of urban demand. Toggle the "Full Cycle" to observe the later pivot: in 2023-24 and 2024-25, that share falls to about 3%, showing a valve that scales up during stress and retracts when less engineered supply is delivered.

The Survival Cycle

This final "Resilience Scan" combines storage percentage with desalination delivered. To read this matrix, use the centred crosshairs (70% storage, 75 GL desalination delivered) as guide lines:

  • Stable Storage (Bottom-Right): the 2012-2015 points sit here. Toggle the return years to see the 2023-2025 points return to the same quadrant.
  • Unbuffered Stress (Bottom-Left): the 2016-2019 points show low storage with limited desalination delivery.
  • Engineered Buffer (Top-Left): the 2020 point is the first high-desalination response while storage remains below the 70% guide line.
  • Supply Buffer (Top-Right): the 2021-2022 points show high desalination continuing while storage improves.

The Victorian Water Descent converges on one conclusion: Victoria's security is still tied to rain and reservoirs, but engineered supply can become a major stabiliser when natural storage falls.

IDW SPATIAL MODEL
Thinking Process

The objective was to establish the spatial extent of the rainfall deficit, as aggregate state-wide averages obscure regional variation. Station-level inputs were interpolated into an Inverse Distance Weighting (IDW) spatial surface using distances between 6 primary weather hubs and 2,957 suburb centroids. The resulting deficit gradient places the strongest values in the north-west and lower values in the south-west.
